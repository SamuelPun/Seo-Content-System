# Content Device Library
*Original content formats that go beyond information coverage*

Use this library when building an article outline. Select 2–4 devices that fit the angle, the reader, and the topic. Not every device suits every article — pick based on what will genuinely serve the reader and differentiate the piece.

---

## Scenario & Character Devices

### 1. Fiction Character Walkthrough
Create a named character with specific, realistic details (income, location, timeline, situation). Walk through their exact problem step by step using real numbers. Readers recognise themselves in the character and follow the logic more easily than abstract explanation.
*Best for:* process-heavy topics, financial or legal subjects, anything where "it depends on your situation" is the default unhelpful answer.

### 2. Before / After Comparison
Same person, two different decisions or approaches. Show the concrete difference in outcome — financial, practical, emotional. The contrast does the persuasive work without the article having to argue explicitly.
*Best for:* topics where there is a clearly better and worse approach, decision-making articles.

### 3. The Worst Case
What happens if someone gets this completely wrong? Describe it in concrete, specific terms — not vague warnings. Most articles tell people what to do. Few tell them exactly what failure looks like.
*Best for:* risk-heavy topics, compliance, health, finance, anything where the downside is real and underappreciated.

### 4. The Best Case
What does the optimal outcome actually look like, with real numbers or specifics? Most articles describe the average path. Showing the ceiling gives readers something to aim at.
*Best for:* topics where significant upside is possible but rarely quantified.

### 5. Year-in-the-Life Timeline
Month by month (or week by week, or stage by stage) — what does this person actually have to do, and when? Converts abstract process into a concrete schedule.
*Best for:* processes that unfold over time, onboarding, compliance calendars, project planning.

## Argument & Perspective Devices

### 6. Common Misconception Bust
"Most people think X. Here is why that is wrong." Name the misconception specifically, explain where it comes from, then correct it with evidence. Readers feel informed rather than lectured.
*Best for:* any topic with established myths, topics where bad advice circulates widely, industries where conventional wisdom lags reality.

### 7. The Counterintuitive Take
The thing that sounds wrong but is actually true once you understand the rules. State it plainly upfront, then explain the reasoning. Creates genuine curiosity and is highly shareable.
*Best for:* topics with non-obvious truths, expert-level insight that contradicts beginner assumptions.

### 8. The False Choice
Two options that people assume are their only options — and the third option they are missing. Reframes the decision entirely.
*Best for:* decision-making articles, comparison content, topics where readers feel stuck.

### 9. The Expert Disagreement
Two legitimate schools of thought on the same question. Present both arguments fairly, then assess which holds up better and why. More intellectually honest than most content, which pretends consensus exists where it doesn't.
*Best for:* contested topics, evolving fields, anything where the "right answer" genuinely depends on circumstances.

### 10. The Rule That Changed
Something that used to be true and is no longer true — and why people are still acting on outdated information. Positions the article as current and the reader as newly informed.
*Best for:* topics affected by regulation changes, market shifts, new research, or evolving best practice.

### 11. The Hidden Trade-off
Every decision has a cost that nobody mentions. Name it explicitly. Readers feel respected for being told the full picture rather than a sales pitch.
*Best for:* product or service comparisons, strategy articles, any topic where optimising one thing creates a problem somewhere else.

## Conversation & Voice Devices

### 12. Expert / Client Dialogue
A roleplay conversation between a practitioner and someone with the reader's problem. Include the questions people are embarrassed to ask. Dialogue format is easier to read than dense prose and models the kind of conversation readers wish they could have.
*Best for:* professional services topics, anything where readers feel intimidated or out of their depth.

### 13. Real Questions from Real People
Not PAA questions — the messier, more specific questions people ask in forums, Reddit, and communities. These reveal what the official sources miss. Answer them directly.
*Best for:* topics with active online communities, anything where the official guidance doesn't match real-world experience.

### 14. The Insider Framing
Information that is technically public but rarely stated plainly. Framed as: "here is what people in this field actually know, that most guides do not say." Does not require revealing secrets — just saying clearly what others say cautiously.
*Best for:* professional or regulated industries, topics where practitioners know things that generic content glosses over.

### 15. Letter to My Past Self
Written from the perspective of someone who has been through the experience — what they wish they had known at the start. Warm, honest, and highly relatable. Works best when the voice is specific and the regrets are concrete.
*Best for:* experience-based topics, life events, anything with a clear before/after journey.

## Data & Evidence Devices

### 16. The Actual Numbers
Take a realistic scenario and run the maths explicitly, showing the workings. Most articles say "you could save significantly" — showing the actual calculation builds far more trust and usefulness.
*Best for:* financial topics, anything where quantification is possible, topics where vague claims dominate.

### 17. The Decision Table
Not a generic feature comparison — a decision table structured around the reader's situation. Rows are situations or circumstances. Columns are the recommended approach for each. "If your situation is X, then do Y."
*Best for:* topics with multiple valid answers depending on circumstances, comparison content, advice that varies by reader type.

### 18. The Reframing Statistic
One specific data point that changes how you think about the whole topic. Lead with it, then build the article's argument around what it means.
*Best for:* topics where the scale or reality of the problem is underappreciated, any article that needs an arresting opening.

## Original Analysis Devices

### 19. The Ignored Audience
Every popular guide is written for the average case. Identify a specific audience segment or situation that no ranking page addresses directly — and write for them. This is the device most aligned with a strong angle.
*Best for:* any topic where the mainstream content ignores edge cases, minority situations, or specific demographics.

### 20. The Checklist With Reasons
Not just "do these 10 things" — but why each one matters and what goes wrong if you skip it. The reasons are what make it original. Anyone can produce a checklist; the reasoning is what no other article has.
*Best for:* process and how-to content, compliance and preparation topics, any article where a checklist format is appropriate.

### 21. The Expert Take
State a clear position on a contested or nuanced question — the kind of position that standard guides are too cautious to take. Back it with reasoning and evidence. Readers who are tired of "it depends" will find this genuinely useful.
*Best for:* topics where genuine expertise exists and conventional content hedges excessively.

### 22. The Gap Nobody Talks About
Identify something real and important that every existing guide omits — a scenario, a risk, a consideration, a step. Name it explicitly. This works best when the gap is genuinely significant, not manufactured.
*Best for:* mature topics where the standard content has converged on the same structure and the gaps are real.

---

## How to choose devices for an article

1. Read `angle.md` — the target reader and their core problem will suggest which devices will resonate
2. Aim for 2–4 devices per article — too many fragments the piece
3. Prioritise devices that produce content no competitor can copy (character walkthroughs, real numbers, expert takes)
4. Avoid devices that feel forced for the topic — a Year-in-the-Life works for a process; it does not work for a conceptual explainer
5. Place devices in sections where the reader most needs a new way of engaging with the information — not just at the start
